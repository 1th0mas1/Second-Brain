# Sturm-Liouville Theory and Generalised Fourier Series

## Function space and generalised Fourier series

The set of functions $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb C$ with $\int_a^b|f(x)|^2w(x)\,dx$ finite forms a vector space $L_w^2(a,b)$, with inner product $\langle f,g\rangle=\int_a^bf^*(x)g(x)w(x)\,dx$ (the weight $w(x)$ playing the role of the metric $M$ in $\underline a^\dagger M\underline b$). Given a complete orthogonal set of basis functions $e_i(x)$ (sines/cosines, Legendre polynomials, Bessel functions, Hermite polynomials — whichever Sturm-Liouville problem is relevant), any $f$ in the space expands as a **generalised Fourier series**
$$f(x)=\sum_ia_ie_i(x), \qquad a_i=\langle e_i,f\rangle$$
by projection, exactly as ordinary vector components are found by dotting with basis vectors. The quality of a finite truncation is measured by $D^2(f,f_n)=\langle f-f_n,f-f_n\rangle$, and **Parseval's identity** $\|f\|^2=\sum_i|a_i|^2$ is Pythagoras' theorem for function space.

## Ordinary Fourier series as the periodic case

For periodic functions on $[-\pi,\pi]$ (or period $L$), the natural orthonormal basis is $e_m(\theta)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{im\theta}$ with weight $w=1$, giving the familiar complex Fourier expansion $f(\theta)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_nc_ne^{in\theta}$, or equivalently the real sine/cosine form $f(\theta)=\frac{a_0}2+\sum_n[a_n\cos n\theta+b_n\sin n\theta]$. This is just the $L^2(-\pi,\pi)$ special case of the general construction — the same projection machinery applies unchanged when the basis is Legendre polynomials on $(-1,1)$ or Bessel functions on a disc.

## Sturm-Liouville form and Hermiticity

A **Sturm-Liouville problem** writes a linear 2nd-order ODE as an eigenvalue equation for an operator
$$A_{SL}\bullet = \frac{1}{w(x)}\frac{d}{dx}\left(p(x)\frac{d\bullet}{dx}\right)+q(x)\bullet, \qquad A_{SL}e_n = \lambda_ne_n$$
with respect to the weighted inner product $\langle f,g\rangle=\int_a^bf^*(x)g(x)w(x)\,dx$. $A_{SL}$ is Hermitian provided the boundary term $[v^*p\,u']_a^b=0$ vanishes — a condition on the boundary conditions imposed on the eigenfunctions, not automatic. Hermiticity then guarantees, exactly as for matrices: real eigenvalues $\lambda_n$, orthogonal eigenfunctions $\langle e_i|e_j\rangle=0$ for $i\ne j$, and completeness — any $f\in L_w^2(a,b)$ expands as a generalised Fourier series $f(x)=\sum_ia_ie_i(x)$, $a_i=\langle e_i|f\rangle$.

## Special functions as eigenfunctions

Each standard special-function family solves a Sturm-Liouville problem generated by separating a PDE in the matching coordinate system:
- **Legendre's equation** $[(1-x^2)u']'=-l(l+1)u$ (angular part of Laplace's equation in spherical polars, $x=\cos\theta$) has polynomial solutions $P_l(x)$ only when $\lambda_l=-l(l+1)$ — regularity at $x=\pm1$ quantises $l=0,1,2,\dots$, with orthogonality $\int_{-1}^1P_lP_m\,dx=\tfrac{2}{2l+1}\delta_{lm}$.
- **Bessel's equation** (cylindrical polars) and **Hermite's equation** $u''-2xu'=-2nu$ (harmonic oscillator, natural domain $(-\infty,\infty)$ since $p(x)=e^{-x^2}\to0$ there) behave the same way: series-solution termination forces quantised eigenvalues, giving $J_m(x)$ and $H_n(x)$ respectively.

This is the general mechanism: imposing physical boundary conditions on a 2nd-order ODE doesn't just pick a solution, it quantises the eigenvalue and produces a complete orthogonal basis for expanding arbitrary functions in that geometry.
