# Groups, Symmetry Transformations and Representations

## Group Axioms and Examples
A **group** $(G,\cdot)$ is a set with a binary operation satisfying four axioms: closure (the product of two elements is in $G$), associativity ($(a\cdot b)\cdot c=a\cdot(b\cdot c)$), existence of an identity $e$ with $e\cdot a=a$ for all $a$, and existence of an inverse $a^{-1}$ for every $a$. **Abelian**: multiplication also commutes ($a\cdot b=b\cdot a$) — not required by the axioms, so most interesting physical symmetry groups (like rotations in 3D) are non-abelian. The **order** of a group is its number of elements, which can be finite or infinite (discrete or continuous). Standard examples: $(\mathbb Z,+)$ (infinite, abelian); the cyclic group $C_N$ of $N$th roots of unity, generated by $g=e^{2\pi i/N}$ under multiplication (finite, abelian); the permutation group $S_N$ of all reorderings of $N$ objects (finite, non-abelian for $N\ge3$, since e.g. swapping objects 1↔2 then 2↔3 differs from doing it in the reverse order).

## Orthogonal Groups and Physical Motivation
**$O(n)$**: the set of orthogonal $n\times n$ matrices, defined by $M^{-1}=M^T$ — this set forms a group under matrix multiplication (closure follows since $(M_1M_2)^{-1}=M_2^{-1}M_1^{-1}=M_2^TM_1^T=(M_1M_2)^T$). Taking the determinant of $M^TM=I$ gives $(\det M)^2=1$, so $\det M=\pm1$. **$SO(n)$** is the subgroup restricted to $\det M=+1$ — pure rotations, excluding reflections (which have $\det M=-1$ and cannot be continuously deformed to the identity). In 2D, $SO(2)$ matrices are exactly the familiar rotation matrices $R(\theta)$, parametrised by a single continuous angle — the simplest example of a continuous (Lie) group.

The physical motivation for this whole framework: the symmetries of a physical law (transformations under which the law's form is left unchanged) always compose consistently (doing symmetry A then B is itself a symmetry) and invert consistently (undoing a symmetry is also a symmetry) — which is precisely the group structure above. This is the mathematical foundation underlying Noether's theorem, where each continuous symmetry group of a Lagrangian generates a corresponding conserved quantity.

## Definition
A **representation** of an abstract group $G$ assigns to each element $g$ an invertible linear operator — a matrix $M(g)$ acting on some finite-dimensional vector space — such that composing group elements corresponds to composing matrices:
$$M(g\cdot g') = M(g)M(g')$$
This structure-preserving map is a **homomorphism**; it need not be injective or surjective (different group elements can map to the same matrix, and not every matrix in the target space need be hit). Two consequences follow directly from the group axioms: taking $g'=e$ (identity) forces $M(e)=I$, and taking $g'=g^{-1}$ forces $[M(g)]^{-1}=M(g^{-1})$ — so the representation automatically respects inverses as well as products.

## Worked Example and Physical Role
The abstract group of 2D rotations is defined purely by its composition rule, $R(\theta)R(\theta')=R(\theta+\theta')$ (rotate by $\theta$ then $\theta'$ is the same as rotating by $\theta+\theta'$); the matrix group $SO(2)$ *is* a representation of this abstract group, with $M(\theta)=R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$. One can verify directly that $R^TR=I$, so $R$ preserves inner products (hence lengths and angles) — exactly the geometric property a rotation should have, and a consistency check that this matrix assignment is a faithful representation of "rotation" as an abstract operation.

More generally, representation theory is the machinery explaining *why* physical quantities transform in definite, classifiable ways under a symmetry group: scalars transform under the trivial (1-dimensional) representation, vectors under the defining representation, and more exotic objects like spinors under representations that don't correspond to any classical picture at all. The same logic — building representations from an abstract group's composition rule — is exactly how the irreducible representations of $SU(2)$ classify angular momentum and spin states in quantum mechanics.
