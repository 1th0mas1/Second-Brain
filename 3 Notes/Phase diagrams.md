# Phase diagrams

- used to delineate where in temperature-pressure space a substance is a stable gas, liquid or solid
![[Pasted image 20261001122217.png|538]]
- phase boundaries are clear between:
	- gas (stable at high temperature, low pressure)
	- liquid (stable at high temperature, high pressure)
	- solid (stable at low temperature, high pressure)
- there are two key locations on the phase diagram:
	- **triple point** where solid, liquid and gas can co-exist
	- **critical point** where the distinction between liquid and gas disappear
	- for water critical point occurs at $T=647.1\text{ K}{}$ and $p = 221 \text{ bars}{}$ clearly beyond conditions seen on earth
	- past critical point species become miscible (eg water with hydrogen)
		- matters for interiors of water-rich exoplanets
- mars ($0.006$ bar, $210$ K) sits near water's triple point
- many species condense somewhere: $\text{N}_{2}$, $\text{CH}_{4}$, $\text{NH}_{3}$, $\text{H}_{2}\text{O}$, salts (NaCl ~1000 K), silicates and metals (>1600 K)
	- condensates differ in density, fall speed, particle size, optical properties

## Latent heat

- **latent heat** denoted $L{}$ is amount of energy released when unit of mass of substance changes phase (holding temperature constant)
	- $Q = mL{}$
		- where $Q{}$ is amount of energy released and $m{}$ is mass of substance
	- *latent heat of vaporization* (or condensation) corresponds to heat required to vaporise a substance from liquid to gas
	- *latent heat of fusion* corresponds to the heat required to change the phase of a substance from solid to liquid
	- *latent heat of sublimation* corresponds to heat required to change the phase of substance from solid to gas
- latent heat can be reasonably assumed to be constant over a broad range of temperature
	- they do decrease with increasing temperature slighly reaching zero at critical point
		- water's only drops ~9% from triple point to near critical point
