# Magnetostatics and Electromagnetic Induction

## Forces and the Biot-Savart Law
A current-carrying wire in a field $\vec B$ feels $d\vec F=Id\vec l\times\vec B$; writing $I=dq/dt$ for an individual charge gives the **Lorentz force** $\vec F=q\vec v\times\vec B$. Since this is always perpendicular to $\vec v$, the rate of work done is $dW=\vec F\cdot\vec v\,dt=q(\vec v\times\vec B)\cdot\vec v\,dt=0$ — magnetic fields never do work on a charge, only redirect it. The field generated by a current element follows the **Biot-Savart law**, $d\vec B=\mu_0I\dfrac{d\vec l\times\hat r}{4\pi r^2}$, integrated over the whole current path (or, using current density $\vec J$ with $Id\vec l=\vec J\,dV$, over a current-carrying volume). This is the magnetic analogue of Coulomb's law for a point charge — same $1/r^2$ falloff, but the cross product means the field circulates around the current rather than pointing radially.

## Dipoles, Divergence and Ampère's Law
A current loop of area $\vec A$ carrying current $I$ has **magnetic dipole moment** $\vec m=I\vec A$, and its far field falls as $1/r^3$, exactly like the electric dipole. It experiences torque $\vec T=\vec m\times\vec B_\text{ext}$ in an external field, and integrating $dW=T\,d\theta$ gives potential energy $W=-\vec m\cdot\vec B_\text{ext}$ — the same structure as the electric dipole, since both derive from a current/charge distribution with zero net monopole term.

Unlike the electric field, $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec a=0$ for *any* closed surface — magnetic field lines never terminate, because there are no magnetic monopoles — giving the differential statement $\nabla\cdot\vec B=0$. For steady currents, **Ampère's law** relates circulation of $\vec B$ to enclosed current: $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec l=\mu_0I_\text{enc}=\mu_0\int\vec J\cdot d\vec a$, or in differential form (via Stokes' theorem), $\nabla\times\vec B=\mu_0\vec J$. This is exact only in magnetostatics — for time-varying charge/current distributions it needs the displacement-current correction (see [[Maxwell's Equations: Capacitance, Displacement Current and EM Waves]]).

## Two Routes to an EMF
There are two physically distinct ways to induce an emf, unified by Faraday's law. First, a wire moving through a static $\vec B$ field: free charges feel the Lorentz force $\vec F=q(\vec v\times\vec B)$, which pushes them along the wire and does work per unit charge $\varepsilon=\int(\vec v\times\vec B)\cdot d\vec l$ — this can be shown to equal $-d\Phi/dt$ for the circuit. Second, and less obviously, a *changing* $\vec B$ field induces an $\vec E$ field even with no moving charges and no wire at all: $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l=-\int\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}\cdot d\vec a$, or in differential (Maxwell-Faraday) form,
$$\nabla\times\vec E = -\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}$$
**Faraday's law** unifies both cases: the induced emf in *any* closed loop equals the negative rate of change of magnetic flux through it, $\varepsilon=-d\Phi/dt=-\frac{d}{dt}\int\vec B\cdot d\vec a$, regardless of whether the flux changes because the loop moves, the field changes, or both. **Lenz's law** is just the minus sign made physical: the induced current always flows so as to oppose the flux change that caused it (energy conservation — otherwise induction would be a free energy source).

## Inductance and Stored Energy
For a single loop carrying current $I$, the flux threading it is proportional to $I$, defining **self inductance** $L=\Phi/I$, so a changing current induces a back-emf $\varepsilon=-L\,dI/dt$ opposing the change (e.g. a long solenoid has $L=\mu_0N^2A/l$). Two coupled circuits have **mutual inductance** $M_{12}=\Phi_1/I_2=M_{21}=\Phi_2/I_1$ — a changing current in one induces emf in the other; this reciprocity ($M_{12}=M_{21}$) underlies transformer operation, where $V_s/V_p=N_s/N_p$ for an ideal (fully-coupled) transformer.

The energy stored in building up a current against the back-emf is $U=\int VI\,dt=\int L\frac{dI}{dt}I\,dt=\tfrac12LI^2=\tfrac12\Phi I$ — independent of circuit geometry. For a solenoid, substituting $L$ and $B=\mu_0NI/l$ gives $U=\tfrac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2\times(\text{volume})$, which generalises to any field configuration as an energy *density* result: $U=\dfrac{1}{2\mu_0}\displaystyle\int B^2\,dV$ — the magnetic analogue of the electric field energy density $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$.
